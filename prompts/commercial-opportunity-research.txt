1. Establish the decision rules before researching
State the assumptions and freeze the evaluation criteria before seeing favorite candidates. Distinguish a decision to build a product from a decision to run a paid experiment.
Use the following default weights for coarse comparison:
Criterion	Weight
Evidence of painful, economically meaningful work	20%
Credible distribution and actual buyer access	20%
Operating fit within my time allowance	20%
Buyer-valued differentiation from alternatives	15%
Founder implementation/domain fit	15%
Repeat purchase potential and reliability of delivery	10%


Explain any adjustment before scoring. If later evidence requires changing criteria, preserve the earlier version, explain why, and rerank all finalists. Do not adjust weights to make a favorite win.
Scores are ordinal judgments, not success probabilities. Report confidence separately. Mark unknowns explicitly; an unknown channel is not a good channel, and a low-risk-looking workflow is not measured low support burden. Do not reward technical fit enough to compensate for absent customers or unmanageable operations.

Reserve one agent for independent skeptical review. If resources permit, use an opportunity-discovery agent to challenge the domain allocation and find plausible ideas outside the obvious industries. Agents should not create their own subagents unless you explicitly allocate resources for it.

Require substantive progress updates: new evidence, rejected assumptions, unresolved gates, and changed rankings. Do not simulate elapsed work, pad the run to appear thorough, or confuse long duration with research quality.
3. Generate and deduplicate ideas

4. Screen the whole catalog, then deepen selectively
For every idea, identify credible existing alternatives: direct products, native platform features, specialist service providers, DIY automation, and the current manual process. Do not force two software competitors when the real alternative is a spreadsheet or employee.
Find at least two relevant alternatives when evidence allows. Use explicit source links for researched offerings. Label a hypothesized manual/DIY substitute as an inference. If no direct competitor is located, describe the search scope and retain uncertainty; never call the market empty.
Record actual overlap, not just competitor names. Inspect the relevant pages before claiming features. Check current pricing, availability, plan limits, and API access for finalists. Distinguish announced, beta, trial, and generally available features. Treat conflicting documentation as unresolved rather than selecting the convenient interpretation.
First triage all ideas. Investigate approximately 15–20 promising candidates more deeply, then 3–5 finalists. Adjust these counts to the available research budget. Clearly disclose which rows received broad screening and which received deep investigation. Do not describe 100 brainstorm rows as 100 fully validated markets.
Seek buyer-side evidence as well as vendor pages: real requirements, firsthand workflow accounts, procurement requests, hiring responsibilities, and dated complaints. Forums can provide leads, but promotional posts, anonymous anecdotes, and duplicated claims are weak evidence.
Separate these claims:
- The task exists.
- The task occurs often and costs enough to matter.
- Existing alternatives fail for this buyer.
- This proposed solution is meaningfully better.
- Buyers will pay and return.
- I can reach and serve them profitably.
Evidence for one does not automatically support the others. Vendor existence proves supply, not an opening for me. Never invent interviews, payments, market size, conversion rates, accuracy, or customer access.
5. Challenge finalists before selecting
Freeze a shortlist before adversarial research. Give the skeptic the same candidate briefs and sources without your ranking or preferred winner.
Require the skeptic to find direct feature overlap, inexpensive/native substitutes, platform expansion, weak repeat frequency, acquisition difficulties, access restrictions, founder knowledge gaps, and hidden human work.
Do not reject an idea solely because competitors exist. Test whether it could win through a concrete buyer-valued advantage: channel, setup effort, accepted deliverable, pricing structure, reliability, or a narrower operational requirement. Any advantage remains a hypothesis until supported.
Independently verify each finalist's decision-critical source claims. Subagent source summaries are leads, not sufficient verification. Assess source quality and date; do not use thin AI-generated vendor pages, unsupported ROI claims, or several copies of one claim as independent confirmation.
If a separate ranking agent is available, have it rank the finalists under the frozen criteria before seeing your ranking. Investigate disagreements; majority votes and average scores do not establish truth.
6. Test product loops and business economics
For each finalist, distinguish:
1. Customer value loop: trigger → input → processing → customer review/action → useful result → next purchase.
2. Product improvement loop: consented feedback → fixture/rule improvement → lower error or review cost on the next cycle.
3. Acquisition loop: how the next suitable buyer learns about and purchases it. Do not invent referrals or network effects.
State where AI is useful and where explicit rules or conventional software are better. A feedback loop is not automatically a moat.
Calculate plausible revenue and workload scenarios with labeled assumptions:
- Revenue = paying accounts × realized price, or paid projects × project price.
- Weekly work = fixed maintenance/admin + support + onboarding + selling + review + exception handling.
- Include AI, infrastructure, payment fees, and paid labor in margin estimates; identify unpaid founder labor separately.
Test a bad case as well as a favorable case. Distinguish one-time setup revenue from recurring revenue. Do not declare a five-hour business while omitting customer acquisition or assuming every exception disappears after automation.
7. Make a comparative decision
Choose one of: build, run a paid validation experiment, investigate a specific unresolved gate, or reject the current set. Do not force a winner to satisfy the prompt.
If recommending an experiment, identify a concrete buyer, bounded offer, price hypothesis, required sample inputs, measurable acceptance criteria, and kill conditions. Explain why this is the best next experiment rather than implying it is already the best business.
State which new evidence would reverse your choice. Check whether reasonable changes to the weights or my time/channel assumptions change the winner. Treat close rankings as ties unless substantive evidence separates them.

Save a reusable report when file tools are available. Keep facts, inferences, and proposed test prices visibly distinct. Do not imply you inspected my repositories unless you actually did. Do not contact people, publish, or modify repositories as part of this research unless separately authorized.
